CHAPTER 1 
Explaining Mechanisms vs. Organisms 
Early breakthroughs with respect to explaining mechanisms (e.g., clocks) or 
inanimate things (e.g., colliding billiard balls) have shaped the narratives that 
dominated early science. The scientific explanations of mechanisms were 
based on causal narratives with a strict ordering in time such that causes 
always preceded effects. Thus, explaining an action (an effect or a response) 
involved tracing back along a sequence of actions until you find a satisfying 
explanation (i.e., the primary cause). This resulted in a metaphorical string of 
dominos that is conveniently decomposable in time. That is, it is possible to 
isolate steps in the sequence (pairs of dominos), and it is possible to identify 
local and remote causes of an action. This led to experimental approaches 
that isolated small sequences of actions to test specific hypotheses, with the 
promise that the understanding of these isolated parts (e.g., gears) could be 
combined to explain the entire sequence (e.g., behavior of the clock). In fact, 
there was an implication that it would ultimately be possible to explain the 
entire universe by isolating the first, primary domino and then tracing forward 
along the causal chain to predict the future.  
It is not too surprising that early biologists and social researchers adopted this 
same narrative in their attempts to develop a scientific approach to explaining 
the actions of organisms. This is clearly illustrated by the stimulus response 
(SR) explanations of behavioral psychologists, or the decomposition of 
cognition into an ordered series of discrete information processing stages. It 
is also why analogies to mechanisms – clockworks, servomechanisms, and 
computers have been accepted as satisfying explanations by many social 
scientists. 
However, it is becoming increasingly evident that the causal narrative that was 
successful for explaining the behavior of mechanisms will not lead to 
satisfying explanations for the behavior of organisms.1  In fact, there is growing 
suspicion that this causal narrative  also falls short with respect to 
understanding complex physical systems (e.g., weather systems2 or dripping 
faucets3). Thus, if the goal is to explain the behavior of organisms it becomes 
necessary to develop an alternative to the causal narrative.

Problems with a Causal Narrative 
Let’s consider some of the reasons why the causal narrative does not lead to 
a satisfying explanation for the behavior of organisms. First, the behavior of 
organisms is shaped by the potential consequences of actions. This is 
reflected in the fact that organisms pursue goals. This does not fit with the 
assumption that causes precede action. Thus, for example, William Powers’ 
Perceptual Control Theory (PCT) 4 suggests that organisms control 
perceptions, not actions. Consider driving down the highway. PCT suggests 
that your actions on the steering wheel are being determined (caused) by the 
perceptions of the position and direction of travel of your car with respect to 
the road (or intended path). In other words, you are not controlling your arms 
or the steering wheel, you are controlling the perceived consequences of 
those actions relative to your intention to keep your car in the proper lane. 
Others might say that the actions are being determined by your intended 
destination, or the ‘why’ of your travel. The broader implication, however, is 
that the coupling between stimuli and actions can be bi-directional or circular. 
In a circle, there is no longer a fixed precedence in time – there is no sense in 
which any processing stage is necessarily prior to any other stage in the circle.  
 
This circular coupling can be represented by adding feedback to the typical 
information processing model of cognition. However, early representations of 
information processing with feedback tended to preserve a fixed ordering in 
time consistent with a causal narrative in which sensing precedes perception, 
which precedes decision making, which precedes action. This  implicit 
precedence is inconsistent with the circular dynamic that results from adding 
feedback.  
In the circle there is no sense in which any information processing stage 
necessarily precedes any other stage. For example, Gibson6 observed that feet 
were a critical component of visual perception because movement is 
necessary to create the visual flow fields that specify our relations to the 
surfaces in our ecology. Figure 1.1 below shows the conventional

A second problem with the mechanistic narrative is that there is an implication 
that the transfer functions of the elements (e.g., the behavior of dominos or 
information processing stages) are fixed. This implies that pairs of dominos or 
stages of processing can be isolated and studied independently from the 
other dominos or stages, and that eventually the behavior of an organism 
could be explained by combining knowledge obtained by studying the 
independent elements. For example, it implies that decision making could be 
understood independently from sensing, perception, or action. However, 
research on Naturalistic Decision Making7 illustrates that decision making is 
not independent from recognition stages (e.g., selective attention and framing 
of the situation). And research on perception and action illustrates that a 
primary function of perception is to tune into the information that specifies the 
opportunities or constraints on action (i.e., the affordances)8.

The need for the stages to interact and change (i.e., to learn or adapt) was  
recognized by Piaget9 with respect to cognitive development and by Boyd10 
with respect to the development of skill or expertise. Piaget described two 
dynamics with respect to cognitive processes – assimilation and 
accommodation. Figure 1.2 illustrates parallels between Piaget’s theories of 
cognitive development and Boyd’s model of creation and destruction in the 
OODA loop. Note that in most prior representations of cognitive processes the 
ecology or situation is implicit.  However, just as it is important to consider the 
topology in order to predict the flow of a river, it is important to consider the 
ecology in order to predict the flow of cognition.  
Assimilation is reflected in the forward loop. This reflects how internal 
schemas (intentions and expectations) guide our interactions with the 
ecology. That is, the schema directs our attention and helps us to guess what 
actions will lead to satisfying interactions with our ecology. For Boyd this is 
analogous to creative action (creation). If our guesses are correct, then the 
resulting feedback will confirm or reinforce the schema as a satisfying framing 
for productive action. 
However, sometimes we will be surprised. That is, the consequences will not 
be consistent with our expectations. The upper loop reflects what Piaget 
called accommodation and what Boyd called destruction. The feedback in 
this loop changes our schema (e.g., it adapts or destroys our old model or old 
assumptions to accommodate the unexpected consequences). That is, the 
transfer function for the box labeled schema is not fixed or stationary. The 
transfer function changes as a result of surprising consequences. This reflects 
the capacity of organisms to adapt or learn from experience. Piaget referred to 
this adaptive learning process as equilibration. It reflects the ability to seek a 
balance or equivalence between what we expect to happen (assimilation) and 
what actually happens (accommodation). Ideally, the equilibration processes 
lead to a continually improving attunement to the pragmatic demands of one’s 
ecology.

Thus, organisms are more complex than simple servomechanisms  or 
computers with fixed transfer functions specified by the engineer or 
programmer. They are engaged in a constant process of attunement to create 
a stable coupling with the ecology. In other words, the survival of organisms 
depends on learning about and adapting to their ecology. A critical insight here 
is that stability is a constraint on the animal/ecology system (i.e., the coupling 
of organisms with their environment). This reverses the narrative with respect 
to parts and wholes. Rather than the whole being the sum of the parts, in a 
circular dynamic, the parts must satisfy the stability constraints on the whole 
circuit, which includes the ecology. For example, if the ecology changes, the 
organism must also change in a way that maintains stability. Otherwise, the 
organism will become extinct. In other words, survival of the organism 
depends on maintaining a stable coupling with its ecology – the transfer 
function of the organism must change (adapt) to meet the stability constraints 
of the coupling.

McRuer’s crossover model11 of how humans adapt to changing dynamics in a 
tracking task is a great illustration of how the transfer function of a human 
tracker must change to meet the demands of stability. If the transfer function 
of the plant is a simple gain (position control), then the human transfer 
function must have the properties of an integrator. But if the plant transfer 
function is an integrator (1st-order, velocity control), then the human transfer 
function must have the properties of a gain. The combined dynamic of human 
plus plant must meet the stability requirements of the entire loop (i.e., must 
be a low-pass filter). Thus, the human transfer function must change to 
complement changes in the plant dynamic. Otherwise, the system (human + 
plant) will be unstable. (this will be discussed in more depth in the 
Controllability Chapter).  
You could also think about this in the context of competitive sports like tennis. 
To be consistently successful against different opponents it is necessary to 
adapt your style of play (your transfer function) in a way that counters the 
different strengths and weaknesses of each of your opponents. Or consider 
what happens when you drive a strange car that might have worse or better 
brakes than the car you drive routinely. Your first attempt to apply the brakes 
in the unfamiliar vehicle might have a surprising consequence. However, you 
are able to quickly accommodate to the new brakes, so that after a few 
attempts you can brake smoothly. Essentially, you are adjusting your own 
‘transfer function’ to achieve a comfortable drive (i.e., stable control).   
Another aspect of the dynamic of circles that is inconsistent with the causal 
narrative is that the dynamics of circles cannot be dissected into a series of 
discrete instances in time (e.g., one domino or billiard ball hitting another in a 
series of discrete collisions). There is not a single  discrete signal in the 
information processing circle that then moves from one stage to the next. 
Rather, all stages are getting signals continuously. For example, you don’t have 
a sensation that becomes a perception that then drives a decision which then 
becomes an action, leading to the next perception, etc. Rather, the organism 
is continuously sensing, which is continuously being integrated into ongoing 
perceptions, that informs ongoing decisions, that are integrated into ongoing 
actions. Further, the constraint on stability is not in time, it is over time. In 
other words, organisms are composed of continuous, analog processes. 
Again, consider driving, you are continuously monitoring conditions, 
assessing the potential risks, and acting to keep within the safe field of travel.

Finally, organisms are not processing single signals in one or two loops. Rather 
organisms are simultaneously dealing with a multitude of signals that are 
percolating through a multilayered network with many loops. This includes 
sensing through multiple modalities, perceiving multi-dimensional situations, 
and directing and coordinating the actions of multiple effectors relative to a 
range of short- and long-range goals and values. Thus, organisms are not 
reacting to discrete particles like colliding billiard balls. Rather, they are 
managing fields of information and coordinating fields of effectors. In motor 
control, this is reflected in the degrees of freedom problem12 – how to produce 
consistent outcomes under varying situations. For example,  coordinating 
multiple muscles and joints to consistently achieve the same ends (e.g., an 
accurate throw to first base) under a wide range of different situations (e.g., 
reflecting different postures resulting from the need to first retrieve a ground 
ball).  
 
The bottom line is that much of the biological and social sciences are framed 
within a causal narrative that was designed to explain inanimate mechanisms. 
  
A New Narrative 
Thus, it is becoming increasingly obvious to many researchers that the 
classical causal narrative does not lead to satisfying explanations for how 
organisms behave. Today a new narrative is being created based on the 
capacity of the organism to resiliently adapt to situated constraints. Rather 
than looking for causes, researchers are beginning to explore how behavior is 
shaped by constraints on perception and action. They are asking how patterns 
in fields of information (e.g., perceptual invariants) specify the opportunities 
and risks for action (e.g., affordances), relative to the successful pursuit of 
satisfying ends (e.g., healthy consequences, stable balance with the ecology, 
survival).13

The intention of this book is to reframe the narrative about the behavior of 
organisms and social organizations in a way that is more consistent with the 
dynamics of circular or coupled systems. The term ‘muddling, ’ that was coined 
by Lindblom14 to describe the way political organizations make decisions, will 
be used as a label for the underlying dynamic.  
In thinking about muddling, here are three seeds to keep in mind: 
• Organisms learn from their mistakes. 
• An organism never steps into the same stream twice.  
• General principles are derived from specific events, while specific 
events are understood in the context of general principles.15 
The first two seeds help to describe the challenge that organisms face. While 
they learn through the errors and surprises that are experienced in the past, 
what they learn must be generalized and applied through actions in a future 
that may be different from the past in important ways. For example, consider 
sports competitions where athletes must eventually face opponents who 
have different strengths and who are changing their own skills in a quest to 
gain a competitive advantage. This challenge is also recognized by military 
science which warns against the danger of ‘fighting the last war. ’ Again, 
recognizing that future opponents are likely to adopt very different tactics and 
strategies than prior opponents. In sum, the point is that our learning will 
generally be tested under conditions that are different than the conditions  
existing at the time we did the learning.  
The third seed suggests that to generalize and apply what is learned from the 
past to the future it is necessary to go beyond the details of specific situations 
to extract more general patterns, heuristics, standards, or principles. 
Addressing the challenge of a constantly changing future requires multiple 
layers of understanding. It suggests that learning requires some degree of 
abstraction in order to apply what was learned from the errors and surprises 
from the last tennis match (or the last war) to the next.  
Today a new narrative is being created based on the capacity of the organism to 
resiliently adapt to situated constraints. Rather than looking for causes, researchers 
are beginning to explore how behavior is shaped by constraints on perception and 
action. They are asking how patterns in fields of information (e.g., perceptual 
invariants) specify the opportunities and risks for action (e.g., affordances), relative 
to the successful pursuit of satisfying ends (e.g., healthy consequences, stable 
balance with the ecology, survival).

The point is that muddling refers to the process of abstracting from the past to 
make smart hypotheses about the future! But it is important to realize that 
even smart hypotheses are guesses, and that errors and surprises are 
inevitable. Yet, despite a limited ability to forecast the future, people can often 
muddle through to achieve some degree of satisfaction in their lives.   
A Next Step 
In the next few chapters, some observations from the design of control 
systems and filters will be explored. Although it is a mistake to think that 
organisms are servomechanisms or even more sophisticated engineered 
control systems, learning about the dynamics of engineered control systems 
and filters is a useful first step to escaping from the traditional causal narrative 
that most of us have grown up with. This is a first step toward understanding 
organisms as self-designing systems.16 
For the most part, mechanisms don’t muddle, at least not to the extent that 
organisms muddle. However, the engineers designing autonomous or semi-
autonomous systems do extensive muddling in the design process to create 
mechanisms that will stably perform in well-defined situations. Thus, one can 
gain a lot of insight into the nature of the muddling processes of organisms 
from considering how engineers addressed the equilibration problems for 
their systems. Specifically, we will look at the problem of observability in the 
next chapter; the problem of controllability in the following chapter; and then 
we will consider the need to coordinate observability and controllability to 
satisfy a design intent.
