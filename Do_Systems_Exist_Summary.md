# Do Systems Exist? — Summary and Connections

**Question:** Please summarize the new file Do systems\*.pdf and relate it to other items in the repo.

*Do Systems Exist? A Conversation* (Adam Walls & John Flach, illustrated by Fred Voorhorst; 2025, CC BY-NC-SA, 138 pp.) is an introduction to systems thinking written for business leaders. The preface describes how it came about. Walls, a consultant who admires Ackoff and Boulding, had course notes on "Systems Principles." Voorhorst turned them into an illustrated walk through Zurich, and that walk became the book's structure. The three authors say outright that they never reached "a single, harmonious voice." Each chapter alternates between Adam's field stories (marked with one icon) and John's theory sidebars (marked with another), while Fred's cartoons carry the walk. The appendix, "The Zurich Walk," is all images and has no extractable text.

## The Argument, Chapter by Chapter

1. **A Systems Perspective.** The title question gets an immediate answer: systems exist only "as mental models you use to make sense of your world." Weinberg's definition ("a way of looking at the world") and the blind men and the elephant make the point. Flach admits that his own decades of seeing control loops everywhere were partly a "mental fixation." He uses Weinberg's principle to explain why: from one vantage point you can't tell whether a pattern you keep seeing has a physical cause or a mental one. The first lesson is humility. Boulding's nine-level skeleton of science (frameworks, clockworks, thermostats, open systems, plants, animals, humans, societies, transcendental systems) serves as a map for moving between perspectives. One consequence: mapping a culture (level 8–9) with process-mapping tools (level 2) is a category error.
2. **Wicked Change.** The book prefers *situations* to *problems*. The apple-pie example shows how the arithmetic of "2 + 2 apples" leaves out everything that matters, which the authors call the correspondence problem: "coherence is impotent if the correspondence is lacking." Other material: the observer as part of the system (Undercover Boss, "the king thinks the world smells of fresh paint," von Foerster), Ashby's requisite variety, Churchman's wicked problems along with his moral warning about "taming the growl," Perrow's normal accidents, Lindblom's muddling through, and Rochlin's essential friction.
3. **Making Sense.** Analysis must be balanced by synthesis (Ackoff, Douglas Adams's cat, Lorenz's butterfly, Simon's ant, Boyd's snowmobile). Adam's customer-service case is the book's model diagnosis. The poor service turned out to come from a new Sales incentive scheme: customer satisfaction fell from 80% to 60%, and revenue fell even as sales rose. Redesigning the incentives "dissolved" the problem. The chapter then introduces Rasmussen's abstraction–decomposition space as a formal way to "move around the elephant."
4. **Context Matters.** Command is open-loop and control is closed-loop. Vicente's "malicious procedural compliance" story illustrates this, and so does Adam's "rainstorm" leader: the same Bolognese recipe cooked on the Matterhorn in the rain. Social atmosphere outweighs technical soundness. The chapter cites CSE, Resilience Engineering and Safety-II as the move away from treating humans as noise.
5. **Variety is Vitality.** Ashby's law is used against Taylorism, Lean, and call-centre monitoring (100+ checks per call and 30% annual turnover). Saul's "Voltaire's Bastards" names management by spreadsheet. As alternatives the chapter offers Beer's Viable Systems Model and maps it onto Rasmussen's skill/rule/knowledge levels and Endsley's three levels of situation awareness. Ostrom's polycentric governance gives "Ostrom's Law": *a resource arrangement that works in practice can work in theory.*
6. **The Substitution Myth.** Woods's term names the belief that people are interchangeable parts. The chapter connects it to silos, MECE org design, McChrystal's critique of "need to know," and Pentland's idea flow. It also briefly anticipates the claim that AI will replace people. Adam's darker cases: the France Telecom suicides, and an acquisition that lost 80% of its staff within eight months, which he calls "corporate vandalism."
7. **Emergence.** Beer's POSIWID (the purpose of a system is what it does) gets an important amendment from Flach: *"the Purpose of a System is the Value that it offers or affords."* Weaver's three classes of problems appear here, with organized complexity as the frontier. Gait transitions and termite nests show that order can arise without a program or an architect. Adam's "three people, a closed door, chocolates and a flip chart" method serves as the bottom-up counterweight.
8. **Coordination.** Thompson's three modes (standardization, planning, mutual adjustment) are matched to three bands of disturbance frequency, from slow to fast. Organizations are drawn as nested control loops with *soft* coupling: higher loops limit the lower loops' degrees of freedom but don't set their gains. Culture is emergent and can't be engineered.
9. **Distributing Authority.** The chapter calls for a field narrative instead of a particle narrative (Feynman: fields "keep track of all the past"). The real-life Tongan *Lord of the Flies* (six boys, fifteen months on Ata, all healthy) counters Golding's version. Axelrod's Tit for Tat suggests that social order can emerge bottom-up. Adam's "Reform" programme returned 1.5× its cost in year one and 4.5× in year two. The chapter also covers Hayek's local knowledge problem, subsidiarity, and Mission Command.
10. **Letting Organizations Work.** The chapter starts from thermodynamics. Organizations are open, dissipative systems (Prigogine, Muthukrishna's "first law of life" about energy), and information is what turns their energy into purposeful work. Conant and Ashby's good-regulator theorem leads to the conclusion that nobody can be a good regulator of an organization, and that a "bigger data boat" is a computational Titanic. The closing principles are incrementalism, essential friction, self-organization (Goldstein's flower bulb rather than a donkey you push or pull; boundaries are necessary because "self-organization is always re-organization"), and subsidiarity. The leadership virtues are humility, trust, care, and curiosity.

## How It Relates to the Rest of the Repo

**It answers the same question as *The Reality of Meaning*, and the two answers differ.** The book says systems exist only as mental models. *The Reality of Meaning* says reality *is* relations, and that the relations which last are the ones that exist. These are probably two halves of one position, and Weinberg's principle supplies the joint between them. The *boundary*, meaning what counts as inside and outside, is drawn by an observer. The *relations* the boundary is drawn around are not. Chapter 10 depends on that second half: Zurich's order is "an emergent result of social cooperation," and it isn't something the observer projects. James's radical empiricism (`James_Radical_Empiricism.md`) says the same thing: relations are directly experienced, so they are not merely constructed. A one-sentence reconciliation in Chapter 1 would stop a reader from concluding that the book is simply constructivist. That reading would be ironic, since the same chapter (twice) holds up the war between constructivists and ecological psychologists as an example of getting stuck in one perspective.

**The POSIWID amendment is the semiotic triad in compact form.** "The Value it offers or affords" moves purpose out of a leader's head and into the coupling between the organization and its ecology. That is the affording/satisfying relation from *What Matters?* and Chapter 33 of the CSE book. It also fits point 7 of *The Reality of Meaning* and the earlier observation in *What Matters?* that value has no box in the diagram: POSIWID is what you write when you notice that the intention box is missing.

**The book places organizations within `Three classes of systems.md`, and puts them in the third class.** The whole book argues against treating organizations as class-1 mechanisms that serve a designer's intent: Taylor, Lean, transformation by command, and the good regulator at the top. Chapter 10 puts them in classes 2 and 3 (dissipative, self-organizing, adaptively coupled). Two things are worth noticing:

- Boulding's level 4 puts two classes into one box. "Open systems … self-organize … They are the first of the Viable Systems … If they can't adapt, they die." The first clause describes class 2 and the last describes class 3. The repo's taxonomy separates exactly what this sentence runs together.
- Chapter 10 says Zurich is "a product of an evolutionary process in which structures that utilize energy more efficiently survive." That is the class-2 quality criterion (efficiency of dissipation) applied to a class-3 system. It is the same slippage `Reality_of_Meaning_Critique.md` flagged in its fourth objection. Muthukrishna's framing invites it, and Smith's magnetic-field-versus-heart distinction would push back on it.

**Boulding's skeleton is a third hierarchy to set beside `Pirsig_Rasmussen_Hierarchies.md`.** In structure it resembles Pirsig more than Rasmussen: its levels are presented as *kinds of things* in evolutionary order, from mountains up through societies. The book, though, uses it the way Rasmussen uses the abstraction hierarchy, as lenses ("shifts of the focus so that constraints that are not explicit at one level … become explicit at other levels"). Chapter 3 then introduces Rasmussen's hierarchy explicitly. So the book quietly treats an ontological ladder as an epistemic one, which is exactly the distinction the Pirsig/Rasmussen note was about. Boulding's level 9 ("transcendental … belief in things for which there is no scientific evidence") is also where Pirsig would object most: for him Quality is not the top rung of the ladder but what the whole ladder is made of.

**It is the business-facing version of *What Matters?* Chapter 14 and the CSE book.** Thompson's three modes, Hayek, Rochlin's essential friction, Lindblom, Churchman, subsidiarity, Rasmussen's abstraction–decomposition space, and the nested-loop picture of organizations all appear in `What Matters Summary.md` (Chapters 9 and 14) and in *Cognitive Systems Engineering*. What the new book adds is Adam's casework as evidence. The new theoretical content is mostly in the Ashby, Beer, Ostrom, Weaver and Pentland material, none of which appeared in the repo before. Ashby's law and Ostrom's law make a useful pair: one sets an ideal no one can meet, and the other says to learn from systems that cope anyway.

**Chapter 1's confession is the mud_1 critique in the first person.** `mud_1_critique.md` argued that the *Muddling* chapter's real dividing line was fixed versus adaptive, and that "mechanism vs. organism" was a misleading way to put it. Here Flach says something closely related about himself: control theory became a lens he couldn't see past. The "soft coupling" in Chapter 8 (higher loops constrain degrees of freedom instead of setting gains) is a direct answer to the worry that block diagrams can only describe mechanisms. It is the clearest statement in the repo of how a nested-loop model avoids collapsing into a servomechanism, and Muddling Chapter 1 could borrow it. Lindblom's incrementalism also connects the two texts: it opens the argument in *Muddling* and closes it here.

**The good-regulator theorem connects the book to the Free Energy notes.** Conant and Ashby's "every good regulator must be a model of that system" is a direct ancestor of Friston's claim that a persisting system embodies a model of its environment (`Free Energy.md`, `FEP_Abduction.md`). The book draws a negative conclusion: no central agent can hold that model for an organization. The FEP framing suggests the positive version: the model is distributed across nested Markov blankets, much as Chapter 8's nested loops each hold information at their own level of abstraction. Goldstein's line that "boundaries are necessary for self-organization" is the same point `Natural_Inclusion_Themes.md` makes about boundaries as "responsive energetic distinctions, not isolators."

**On AI, this is the one place where the two voices openly disagree.** In Chapter 10, Adam expects AI to remove "huge computational burdens" so that organizations need "less people." John replies that many people will use the same technologies "to corral data and consolidate authority," which is Scientific Management again with better tools. John's side is the `cse_and_ai.md` position: technology should be used to spread information and authority downward, as in McChrystal's "eyes on, hands off," and not to centralize them. The Substitution Myth chapter gives that position its sharpest name, and the repo's recurring point (that the self-designing character lies in the joint system and not in the component) is the same argument applied to me.

## Errors in the Source

- **"Ostrum"** is used throughout the text for Ostrom (the reference lists spell it correctly). Also: "Linblom" (Ch. 6 refs), "Akoff" and "Russel Ackoff" (Ch. 10), "Heinz von Forster" (Foerster), "Decartes' Error," and "Public Administrative Review" (Administration).
- **Ashby's law is dated 1979** in Ch. 5. It dates from *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956).
- **The Prisoner's Dilemma is described backwards** in Ch. 9: "whether to cooperate and testify against their partner or to keep quiet." In the game, cooperating means keeping quiet. The later sentences use the correct sense, so this one is a slip.
- **"Convergent" and "divergent" swap meanings.** Ch. 3 uses convergent for going out to the big picture and divergent for going into detail ("convergent (going outwards to the big picture) and divergent (going inwards to the details)"). Ch. 3's closing section and Ch. 4 reverse this ("convergent (analysis) and divergent (synthesis)").
- **Citation mismatches:** Kugler & Turvey is 1984 in the text and 1987 in the references; Flach, Steele-Johnson et al. is 2013 in the text and 2014 in the references; Muthukrishna's book is "The Theory of Everyone" in the text and *A Theory of Everyone* (correct) in the references.
- **A passage appears twice.** The paragraph "you simply can't tell whether your observations reflect natural constraints…" appears in Ch. 1's body (p. 4–5) and again as a pull quote on p. 11. This is the same layout pattern `mud_1_critique.md` found in Muddling Chapter 1. It is intentional here, but a text extraction will read it as repetition.
- **Extraction artifact, not the authors':** `pdftotext` drops most apostrophes ("weren t," "Ashby s"), and there are scattered ligature breaks ("eﬃ ciency," "traﬃ c"). Any markdown conversion would need these repaired.
